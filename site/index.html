Verifying AI Outputs: A Beginner's Manual

Spotting hallucinations, checking sources, comparing answers, and citing facts — in plain English

Published by Asdale Dynamics, LLC

The Golden Rule

Never treat AI as an authority on health, law, or money.

Welcome

You've asked an AI a question. It gave you an answer that looks confident and well-written. But is it true?

This manual teaches you how to check. No technical background needed. Just a willingness to pause and verify.

Chapter 1: Why AI Gets Things Wrong

What Is a Hallucination?

When AI makes something up but sounds completely sure of itself, that's a hallucination. It's not lying on purpose. It's predicting what words should come next based on patterns — and sometimes those patterns lead to fiction.

Common signs:

  • Specific-sounding details that don't exist (fake citations, made-up studies, invented quotes)
  • Confident tone with no hedging ("The study found..." vs "A study might have found...")
  • Internal contradictions within the same answer
  • Claims that sound plausible but can't be verified

Why It Happens

AI models don't "know" facts. They predict likely word sequences. If the training data contains errors, the model learns those errors. If a question falls outside its training, it improvises.

Chapter 2: Spotting Hallucinations — Step by Step

Step 1: Read with Skepticism

Don't skim. Read every claim as if you need to prove it to someone else.

Step 2: Flag Specific Claims

Circle or highlight anything that sounds like a fact: names, dates, numbers, study results, statistics, quotes attributed to real people, legal or medical statements, product specifications.

Step 3: Ask "How Would I Check This?"

For each flagged claim, imagine the simplest way to verify it: a name → search the person + claim; a study → search the title + journal; a statistic → search the number + source; a quote → search the exact phrase in quotes.

Step 4: Search Independently

Open a browser. Don't ask the AI to check itself. Use a search engine or go directly to trusted sites.

Step 5: Compare Results

If multiple independent sources agree, the claim is likely solid. If only the AI says it, treat it as unverified.

Step-by-Step Source Check

  1. Find the original source — Don't trust a summary. Click through to the actual study, law, or document.
  2. Check the date — Is it current? Medicine and law change fast.
  3. Check the author — Are they qualified? A cardiologist writing about heart disease? Yes. A cardiologist writing about tax law? No.
  4. Check for consensus — Do other experts agree? One study ≠ settled science.
  5. Watch for conflicts of interest — Who funded it? Who benefits?
Red Flags in Sources
  • No author listed
  • No date
  • No citations or references
  • Sensational headlines ("Shocking new study reveals...")
  • Selling something related to the claim
  • Only appears on one website

Chapter 4: Comparing Answers — Step by Step

Why Compare?

Different AIs have different training data, different strengths, and different blind spots. Comparing catches errors any single AI might miss.

How to Compare

Step 1: Ask the same question to 2–3 different AIs

Use different platforms (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, local models). Keep the prompt identical.

Step 2: Compare the core claims

Do they agree on the basic facts? Do they cite the same sources? Do they express the same uncertainty?

Step 3: Note disagreements

If AIs disagree on a fact, that fact is unverified. Dig deeper on the disputed points. Don't average them — investigate.

Step 4: Check against non-AI sources

The AIs might all be wrong in the same way. Always verify against primary sources.

Chapter 5: Citing Facts — Step by Step

When You Use AI Output in Your Work

If you include AI-generated information in a report, article, email, or presentation — cite it honestly.

What to Include in a Citation

  1. The AI tool used (name and version if known)
  2. The date you asked
  3. The exact prompt (or a summary)
  4. The specific claim you're citing
  5. Your verification status (verified / unverified / contradicted)

Citation Format Examples

For verified claims:

"According to a 2023 CDC report (verified via CDC.gov), flu vaccination reduces risk by 40–60%. [Generated with ChatGPT-4, Oct 2024; verified independently]"

For unverified claims:

"AI suggested that magnesium improves sleep quality. [Generated with Claude 3, Oct 2024; not independently verified]"

For contradicted claims:

"One AI claimed Study X found Y. Another AI cited Study X finding Z. [Generated with multiple AIs, Oct 2024; sources contradict — do not cite as fact]"

In Academic or Professional Work

Check your institution's policy. Many now require:

  • Disclosure of AI use
  • Separation of AI-generated vs. human-verified content
  • No citing AI as a primary source
